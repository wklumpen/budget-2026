Transit's Conflicting Priorities

Transit provides two different types of service: Frequency service moves lots of people efficiently, and drives ridership. Coverage service ensures that everyone in Calgary is near a stop. Even though these different services have different goals, right now we judge them the same way, usually on productivity.

Right now, our city policies on transit and land use mean that we continue to drift more and more towards coverage-based service. Adding new subdivisions on the edge of the city and not funding our Primary Transit Network properly are two ways that this shift happens.

There is no right or wrong balance of coverage and frequent service, but Calgarians and Council have not been asked how they prefer to balance things. In this page, we explore how that balance works, and how it has changed over time.

Frequent service has not kept pace with coverage

Fall schedules, weekday, 2016 · 2019 · 2026

Most of the drop in frequent service happened between 2016 and 2019, before COVID. Schedules cannot say why. Route restructures (Route 3 split, Circle and BRT North replaced, MAX lines) and stop consolidation distort route-level comparisons, so the trend uses stop-level and area measures. This is service supplied, not ridership: the City does not publish ridership by route.

What could we do differently?

Every hour of service goes to one job or the other. Moving hours between them is a choice, and it has consequences either way.

Lean toward coverage

What we could do

  • Extend routes into new communities as they open.
  • Keep low-use local routes running everywhere they run today.
  • Add evening and weekend hours to existing local routes.
  • Place stops closer together, so everyone is nearer to one.

What follows

  • More of the city is within a short walk of a stop.
  • Ridership will likely shrink.
  • Hours are spread thin. Routes run every 30 to 60 minutes, so trips take planning and a missed transfer is a long wait.
  • Less is left for frequent corridors, which stay as they are or slip.
  • Closer stops mean more stops per trip, so buses run slower and each route needs more hours to run as often.
  • Cost grows with the size of the city, whether or not ridership grows with it.

Lean toward frequency

What we could do

  • Fund the Primary Transit Network: every 10 minutes or better, all day, every day.
  • Pay for it by reshaping the lowest-use local routes, or by directing growth in the transit budget to it.
  • Consolidate stops on frequent routes, so buses spend less time stopping and the same hours can run more often.
  • Ask for frequent service along the corridors where new housing is approved.

What follows

  • Turn-up-and-go service: no timetable to check, short waits for transfers, more places within reach.
  • Ridership will likely grow. Frequency is the service riders respond to most.
  • Fewer stops mean longer walks for some people, and some edge areas would get less service, which hurts people who have no other way to travel.
  • It needs a plan for those areas, such as on-demand service or a longer walk to a better stop.

Neither is wrong. Today we are choosing between them without saying so.

The ask

  1. Budget the two services separately. In the 2027–2030 budget, show a coverage line and a frequency line, and have Council set the balance between them on purpose.
  2. Count the cost to both lines. New community decisions and other major land use changes should include their effect on the coverage line and the frequency line before a decision is made.
  3. Ask Calgarians to help decide the balance. Put the question to the public. Edmonton has.

This is a question about balance, not a call to cut coverage. Administration is best placed to cost the options.

How this is built

  • Hours. Revenue hours are each trip's first-to-last-stop time from the published schedule, split across clock hours. A typical week is four Monday–Thursday days, a Friday, a Saturday and a Sunday. The two one-off October weekend services are left out.
  • Frequency. For each route and hour, trips starting per direction are averaged over that hour and its neighbours, then averaged across the two directions. An hour counts toward frequency service when that rate meets the threshold. A route can provide both types in one day: frequent at noon and coverage at 10 pm.
  • Routes, not corridors. Two routes sharing a street each count separately, so a corridor served every 10 minutes by two 20-minute routes shows as coverage. Use the overrides where you know a route is part of a combined frequent corridor.
  • Deadhead. All service carries an assumed 10% deadhead on top of revenue hours. Because the rate is the same for both types, it changes the totals but not the split. In practice coverage routes probably carry more, since they often run farther from garages with shorter spans, so the coverage share here is if anything a little low.
  • CTrain. CTrain is classified hour by hour with the same frequency rule, and counted in the split. A CTrain hour is a train-hour, which costs more to run and carries far more people than a bus hour, so the operating factor sets how many bus hours one train-hour is worth. Available Calgary figures put it between 2 and 3, so the default is 2.5. It is shown in a darker shade so its share stays visible.
  • What is left out. School specials (routes 600–899) serve a third purpose and are excluded from the split. Ridership, revenue and cost by route are not public, so this measures what the system puts in, not what it gets back.
  • Not the Primary Transit Network. This uses frequency, not the City's PTN corridor designation. The 10-minute mark on the slider is the PTN service standard for comparison.
